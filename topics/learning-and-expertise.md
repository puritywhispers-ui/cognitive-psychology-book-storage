# Learning, skill, and expertise

Cognitive psychology treats learning as changes in knowledge structures and control processes, not only as strengthening of stimulus-response links.

## Useful distinctions

- Declarative vs. procedural knowledge (Anderson ACT-R)
- Controlled vs. automatic processing (Schneider & Shiffrin; later critiques)
- Implicit vs. explicit learning (artificial grammar, sequence learning — interpret cautiously)
- Deliberate practice vs. accumulated experience (Ericsson; later debate on effect sizes and domain limits)
- Transfer: near vs. far; why most training stays narrow

## Problem solving

- Newell & Simon: problem space, operators, heuristics (means-ends, hill climbing)
- Well-defined vs. ill-defined problems
- Insight vs. incremental search; representation change
- Analogical transfer (Gick & Holyoak; Gentner structure-mapping)
- Expertise as chunking and perception of typical structure (Chase & Simon chess)

## Books

- Anderson — *Cognitive Psychology and Its Implications*; ACT-R papers
- Newell & Simon — *Human Problem Solving*
- Ericsson, Charness, Feltovich, Hoffman (eds.) — *The Cambridge Handbook of Expertise and Expert Performance*
- Robertson — *Problem Solving: Perspectives from Cognition and Neuroscience*
- Willingham — *Why Don't Students Like School?* (applied cognitive science for instruction)

## Instructional implications that are actually supported

- Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, feedback timing
- Worked examples early, fading later
- Cognitive load as a design constraint, not a single number
- Expertise reversal: techniques that help novices can hurt experts
