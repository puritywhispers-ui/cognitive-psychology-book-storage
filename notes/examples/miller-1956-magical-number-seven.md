# Miller (1956) — The magical number seven

## Question

Is there a common capacity limit across absolute judgment, span tasks, and related information-processing measures?

## Method in one paragraph

This is a theoretical review plus synthesis, not a single new experiment. Miller gathers absolute-identification results (unidimensional judgments of pitch, loudness, taste, etc.) and memory-span results, then reads them through early information theory (bits).

## Result that matters

Unidimensional absolute judgment often bottoms out around 2.5 bits (~6–7 categories). Immediate memory span for unrelated items often sits near 7. Recoding / chunking can expand the *effective* number of items without expanding the number of chunks.

## Interpretation the authors give

A rough channel-capacity metaphor for immediate processing; chunking as the human workaround.

## Alternative interpretations

Later work split "7±2" into different limits: item limits vs. resource limits vs. focus-of-attention limits (often closer to 4 in Cowan's work). Information-theory bits are not the best currency for all tasks.

## Lasting contribution

Gave cognitive psychology a public capacity number, legitimized information-processing talk, and made *chunking* a permanent concept.

## Limits

The "magical" framing over-unifies distinct tasks. Do not cite "working memory = 7" as current theory.
